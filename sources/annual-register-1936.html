Annual Register, 1936

Annual Register, 1936

Jul 14, 202610 min read

The Annual Register for 1936 (Vol. 178)

Part I — English History

Chapter I — The New Reign

The year opened with the fight of Ramsay MacDonald and his son Malcolm to re-enter Parliament after losing their seats in the 1935 election. Japan withdrew from the Naval Conference on January 16. A coal strike was narrowly averted. On January 20 King George V died at Sandringham, aged 70, after 26 years on the throne. King Edward VIII was proclaimed, and the nation united in mourning. The funeral was at Windsor. The new King broadcast a moving tribute. The rearmament White Paper was published (March), the Rhineland crisis erupted (March 7), and Sir Thomas Inskip was appointed Minister for the Co-ordination of Defence. The naval conference concluded without Japan.

Chapter II — The Failure of Sanctions

Mr. Eden protested at Geneva against Italy’s use of poison gas in Abyssinia. Mr. Baldwin defined Britain’s position: sanctions were slow and lost force without the ultimate threat of blockade. The Budget brought increased taxation. Defence loan was foreshadowed. Sanctions were debated intensely as Italy’s victory in Abyssinia became inevitable. The League of Nations Union sent a deputation. Labour moved a vote of censure. By June, the Government decided to abandon sanctions; Mr. Eden defended the decision. By-elections were lost by the Government. Resignation of Mr. J. H. Thomas; Sir Samuel Hoare became First Lord of the Admiralty. The Budget leakage tribunal reported. Parliament passed the Education Bill and the Tithe Bill. Emperor Haile Selassie arrived in London as an exile.

Chapter III — Britain and the Conflict in Spain

The Spanish Civil War erupted in July. France proposed a non-intervention agreement, which Britain welcomed and extended to Germany, Italy and Portugal. The Non-Intervention Committee was set up in London. Public opinion was deeply divided: Labour and Liberals saw the struggle as democracy against Fascism and pressed for aid to the Spanish Government. The Anglo-Egyptian Treaty was signed (August). Troops were sent to Palestine to quell Arab unrest. The Trades Union Congress rejected a United Front with Communists. The franc devalued; Britain supported it. Conservatives at their conference debated rearmament. Fascist-Communist street violence in London’s East End led to the Public Order Bill.

Chapter IV — Abdication of Edward VIII

Parliament met on November 3 with the King’s Speech setting out a programme including rearmament, non-intervention in Spain, and League reform. The Jarrow March arrived in London. The King toured South Wales’ distressed areas. In December the crisis broke: King Edward VIII wished to marry Mrs. Wallis Simpson, an American divorcée. The Cabinet, led by Mr. Baldwin, rejected the proposal for a morganatic marriage. The Dominions were consulted and concurred. The King chose to abdicate rather than abandon the marriage. On December 10, the Instrument of Abdication was signed, and Parliament passed His Majesty’s Declaration of Abdication Act. Prince Albert succeeded as King George VI. The ex-King broadcast to the nation and left for France. Mr. Eden defended non-intervention as the year closed.

Part I — Imperial History

Chapter I — Ireland (Northern Ireland and Irish Free State)

Northern Ireland enjoyed a year of peaceful progress, with Lord Craigavon beginning his fifteenth year as Prime Minister. The Budget balanced with a surplus. Sectarian disorder did not recur. The Irish Free State saw Ministerial changes. An air route agreement was signed between the UK, Irish Free State, Canada and Newfoundland. The Fianna Fáil party gained by-elections but lost Dublin municipal seats.

Chapter II — Canada

Canada experienced continued economic recovery under Mr. Mackenzie King, who returned as Prime Minister in October 1935. The Dominion faced trade negotiations with the United States. The Bank of Canada commenced operations. Parliament debated imperial preference and defence contributions. The Canadian pilgrimage to London for the King’s funeral was a notable event.

Chapter III — The Union of South Africa

General Hertzog’s government pursued the Representation of Natives Bill, removing the Cape native franchise and substituting communal representation by whites. The Bill aroused fierce controversy. Dr. Malan’s Nationalist Party opposed it as inadequate; the Dominion Party also resisted. South Africa remained on the gold standard. The question of the country’s constitutional status vis-à-vis the Crown continued to simmer.

Chapter IV — Australasia: Commonwealth of Australia and New Zealand

Australia under Mr. Lyons saw economic improvement with rising wool and wheat prices. The government pursued trade diversion policies to protect local industry. New Zealand under the new Labour government of Mr. Michael Joseph Savage began an ambitious programme of social reform, including the forty-hour week, state housing, and guaranteed prices for dairy farmers. The Statute of Westminster was adopted.

Chapter V — India

The Government of India Act of 1935 came into partial operation, with provincial autonomy being prepared. Elections were held for the new provincial legislatures. Congress Party participation remained uncertain. Debates continued on safeguards, the princes’ position in the federal scheme, and communal representation.

Part II — Foreign History

Chapter I — The League of Nations

The League faced the gravest crisis in its history. The Italo-Abyssinian war became a test case for collective security — and the League failed it. Sanctions were abandoned in June. The Abyssinian question was dropped from the Council agenda. The League’s prestige fell worldwide. Germany’s remilitarization of the Rhineland in March further undermined the post-war settlement. The League remained powerless to restrain the Great Powers.

Chapter II — France and Italy

France experienced continuing political instability under the Popular Front government of Léon Blum, elected in May. The Matignon Agreements brought major social reforms: forty-hour week, paid holidays, collective bargaining. The franc came under pressure and was eventually devalued in September. Italy completed its conquest of Abyssinia in May (the fall of Addis Ababa), proclaimed the empire, and King Victor Emmanuel III was named Emperor. Italy grew closer to Germany.

Chapter III — Germany and Austria

Germany under Hitler remilitarised the Rhineland on March 7, tearing up the Locarno Treaties. British and French protests were ineffective. Hitler’s peace proposals were evasive. The Nuremberg Rally was held as usual. The Four-Year Plan was announced for economic autarky. The Berlin-Rome Axis began to take shape. In Austria, Schuschnigg’s government faced intensifying Nazi pressure, isolated after the Italo-Abyssinian war weakened Italy’s protection.

Chapter IV — Soviet Russia and Eastern Europe

The USSR under Stalin adopted a new Constitution (the “Stalin Constitution”) in November, nominally democratic but consolidating one-party rule. The purges intensified, with the first great show trial (Zinoviev-Kamenev) in August. The Soviet-French mutual assistance pact remained unratified by France. Poland, the Baltic states, Czechoslovakia, and the Balkan states sought to balance between Germany and the USSR. Turkey secured the Montreux Convention restoring full sovereignty over the Straits.

Chapter V — Lesser States of Western and Northern Europe

Belgium renounced its military alliance with France (October) and adopted a policy of “independent” neutrality. The Netherlands and Switzerland maintained strict neutrality. In Spain, the Civil War raged after the July military rising led by General Franco. Portugal under Salazar supported the Nationalists. The Nordic states continued their policy of co-operation and neutrality.

Chapter VI — The Middle East: Iran, Afghanistan, Iraq, Palestine, Syria

Iran (formerly Persia) consolidated under Reza Shah. Iraq made progress toward independence. Palestine experienced renewed Arab rebellion against Jewish immigration and land purchases, prompted by the large influx from Germany; British troops were sent to restore order. Syria remained under French mandate with nationalist agitation. The Anglo-Egyptian Treaty (August) regulated the status of the Sudan and British military basing rights, granting Egypt fuller sovereignty.

Chapter VII — The Far East: China, Japan, Netherlands Indies

Japan continued its domination of Manchuria and pressed into North China. The Anti-Comintern Pact was signed between Germany and Japan in November. China under Chiang Kai-shek faced both Japanese encroachment and the continued Communist insurgency. The Netherlands Indies maintained neutrality.

Chapter VIII — Morocco and Egypt

Morocco remained under French and Spanish control. Egypt achieved a new Treaty of Alliance with Britain (signed August 26), regularising the status of the Sudan, British military occupation of the Canal Zone, and Egyptian sovereignty. The treaty was widely welcomed in Egypt.

Chapter IX — America: The United States and Latin America

President Roosevelt campaigned for re-election on the New Deal record and won a landslide victory over Alf Landon in November (46 of 48 states). The US remained isolationist, passing the Neutrality Acts. The Pan-American Conference at Buenos Aires strengthened hemispheric solidarity. In Latin America, the Chaco War between Bolivia and Paraguay formally ended. Brazil under Vargas continued centralisation.

Part II — Chronicle of Events in 1936

Day-by-day record of the year’s notable events: the death of George V and accession of Edward VIII, the Rhineland occupation, the fall of Addis Ababa, the Spanish Civil War outbreak, the Berlin Olympics (August), the Montreux Convention, the Rome-Berlin Axis declaration, and the Abdication crisis.

Retrospect of Literature, Art, and Science in 1936

Literature (by J. Isaacs): Notable works included Margaret Mitchell’s Gone With the Wind, John Maynard Keynes’s General Theory of Employment, and poetry by W.H. Auden. Art (by W.T. Whitley): The Royal Academy and modern British art. Drama (by W.A. Darlington): The London stage with works by J.B. Priestley and the founding of the Old Vic Company. The Cinema (by Monica Ewer): Chaplin’s Modern Times and British documentary films. Music (by W. McNaught): The Proms, works by Vaughan Williams, the death of Elgar. Biological Sciences (by W.B. Brierley): Advances in genetics and vitamins. Physical Sciences: Nuclear physics progress.

Finance and Commerce in 1936

The economic year showed steady recovery: trade improved, unemployment fell from 1.8 million to 1.5 million. The Budget had a surplus. Building activity boomed. Agricultural prices rose. The Stock Exchange rallied. Defence spending increased. The Special Areas (depressed regions) received new assistance.

Law in 1936

Important legal decisions: the Public Order Act curbed political uniforms and paramilitary organisations; the Trunk Roads Act transferred major routes to central government; the Education Act raised the school leaving age; the Cotton Spinning Industry Act reorganised the industry; the Tithe Act commuted tithe rentcharge.

Public Documents

I. The Soviet Constitution (November 1936) — the “Stalin Constitution,” declaring the USSR a socialist state of workers and peasants, guaranteeing civil rights (in theory), and establishing the Supreme Soviet as the supreme legislative body. II. Treaty of Alliance Between His Majesty, in Respect of the United Kingdom, and His Majesty the King of Egypt (August 26) — the Anglo-Egyptian Treaty, regulating the status of the Sudan, British military basing in the Canal Zone, and Egyptian sovereignty; ratified by Parliament. III. Guarantees by Britain to France and Belgium — staff talks and mutual assurances in response to the German remilitarisation of the Rhineland.

Obituary of Eminent Persons Deceased in 1936

Notable deaths included King George V, Rudyard Kipling (writer), G.K. Chesterton (writer and critic), A.E. Housman (poet and scholar), Sir Henry Newbolt (poet), Sir Robert Baden-Powell (founder of the Boy Scouts), Cardinal Bourne (Archbishop of Westminster), and Luigi Pirandello (Nobel Prize-winning playwright).
