The Annual Register for 1934

Part I — English History

Chapter I — The Question of Rearmament

The year opened with a confident New Year message from Prime Minister Ramsay MacDonald, who noted that Britain’s exports had maintained their level despite shrinking world trade and that unemployment was falling. However, two factors forbade complacency: the absence of any revival of world trade and the precarious condition of the Disarmament Conference. The Government’s attention was divided between disarmament negotiations, domestic reforms, and the growing need for rearmament. Mr. Eden was given a new post, Sir J. Simon reported on the Disarmament Conference, and a new German note on armaments prompted a British memorandum. Trade negotiations with France began, Anglo-Japanese textile conversations took place, and the T.U.C. declared against war policy. The Government supported Austrian independence and concluded an Anglo-German debt agreement. An Unemployment Bill was in committee, and the “March of the Unemployed” to London drew parliamentary attention. Anxiety over Fascist movements grew following the Olympia “Blackshirt brutality” incident. The National Government campaign and questions of Cabinet reconstruction featured prominently. Civil, Navy, and Army estimates were presented; slum clearance and overcrowding policy were debated.

Chapter II — The Increase in Air Armament

Parliament reassembled after Easter on April 10, and the Minister of Transport moved the second reading of the new Road Traffic Bill. The Budget was presented by the Chancellor, showing a surplus and its proposed application. The Unemployment Bill reached its concluding discussions. A Cabinet reconstruction took place. The Tithe Bill was introduced and later withdrawn, the Petroleum Bill passed through the Lords, and the Betting Bill was debated. The Iron and Steel industry faced reorganisation, and wage agreements were legislated. The Government responded to Japanese textile competition and the New Zealand tariff offer. A Milk Subsidy was introduced. Lord Salisbury’s House of Lords Bill sparked demands for Lords reform. The Palestine Loan was guaranteed. French notes on “guarantees of execution” and German rearmament prompted Cabinet deliberations, with Signor Suvich visiting London. A British reminder was sent to Japan. The Lords debated the Government’s foreign policy. Peace declarations came from the Labour Movement and the Archbishop of Canterbury. The Finance Bill passed, repealing the Land Value Tax. The “Blackshirt brutality” at Olympia was discussed in the Commons. Reports on housing and the Most-Favoured Nation clause were presented. The dispute over Waterloo Bridge continued. An Anglo-French Trade Agreement was finally reached, and Germany suspended debt payments, leading to a British protest and ultimately a clearing agreement.

Chapter III — The India Report

Parliament rose on July 31 for the summer vacation. The period was marked by concern over a sharp increase in motor accidents on the roads, leading to the Minister of Transport issuing new orders. A railway wage agreement was reached. Lancashire’s trade concerns with both Germany and Australia were aired. The Labour Party published a report on Fascism; the Trades Union Congress debated the issue, and Fascist and anti-Fascist demonstrations clashed in Hyde Park. A report on migration policy was issued. The Queen Mary was launched. A threatened coal strike in South Wales was averted. The Liberal Party announced its election decision. The Labour Party Conference showed hostility to Communists, and Sir Stafford Cripps and the Socialist League suffered defeats. The India Report dominated political discussion, with the future of Indian constitutional reform at the centre of the Government’s programme. The Joint Select Committee on India concluded its work, preparing the ground for the Government of India Bill.

Chapter IV — Help for the Depressed Areas

Parliament reassembled for a new session on November 20. The King’s Speech outlined an unusually large programme of legislation, foremost being proposals for the future government of India. Other major measures included Bills for assisting the tramp shipping industry, preventing overcrowding, and promoting recovery in the depressed areas. The Electricity Supply Act of 1926 was to be amended, and steps were taken for the further development of imperial air communications. The Government also announced plans to assist the depressed areas, addressing the persistent unemployment and social distress in regions like South Wales, Lancashire, and the industrial North-East.

The Empire

Ireland

The Parliament of Northern Ireland passed important legislation, with special attention to agricultural interests and the alleviation of unemployment. The Minister of Finance introduced a Budget estimating expenditure of £11,756,000 for the year.

Canada

Canada made steady progress towards recovery from the economic depression, though hampered by a burdensome railway system and the disposal of accumulated grain crops. A severe drought in the West reduced the aggregate yield of crops, but compensation from better prices helped.

The Union of South Africa

The year opened with the prospect of party fusion brightly in the ascendant. Long-standing causes of schism and suspicion were cleared away, and the result, as General Smuts described it, was the birth of a nation.

Australasia

The Commonwealth of Australia and New Zealand continued their economic recovery, with trade relations with Britain featuring prominently in political debate.

India

The year 1934 in India was one of recovery, adjustment, and preparation for the great constitutional advance for which legislative proposals were being shaped. Civil disobedience, which had been ebbing since 1933, continued to decline, and the “greater quietude” of the previous year was maintained and advanced.

Foreign History

Chapter I — The League of Nations

The year 1934 reflected the alternation of fear, suspense, relief, and hope which characterised the political history of the world. For Europe it was a year of violent shocks and alarms, intensified by bloodshed in many countries, with the ever-present threat that internal unrest might overflow into international anarchy.

Chapter II — France and Italy

In the closing days of December 1933, a financial fraud involving the Municipal Loan Office of Bayonne and an adventurer named Stavisky was discovered. Stavisky’s violent death and the subsequent revelations shook the French Third Republic, leading to the far-right riots of February 6, 1934, the fall of the Daladier government, and the formation of a national unity government under Gaston Doumergue.

Chapter III — Germany and Austria

In 1933 the totalitarian state was established in Germany. In 1934 the tensions within the Nazi Party itself became intense, culminating in the Night of the Long Knives (June 30), when Hitler purged the SA leadership including Ernst Röhm. In Austria, Chancellor Dollfuss was assassinated in a failed Nazi coup (July 25), but the putsch collapsed. President Hindenburg died on August 2, and Hitler assumed the powers of the presidency, becoming Führer and Reich Chancellor.

Chapter IV — Soviet Russia — Estonia — Latvia — Lithuania — Poland — Danzig — Czechoslovakia — Hungary — Rumania — Yugoslavia — Turkey — Greece — Bulgaria

The Soviet Union pressed forward with its Five-Year Plan and collective farming. The Baltic states consolidated their positions. Poland signed a non-aggression pact with Germany. The Little Entente (Czechoslovakia, Rumania, Yugoslavia) faced growing pressure from revisionist powers. Turkey, Greece, and the Balkan states pursued rapprochement through the Balkan Pact.

Chapter V — Lesser States of Western and Northern Europe

Belgium, the Netherlands, Switzerland, Spain, Portugal, Denmark, Iceland, Sweden, Norway, and Finland each contended with the pressures of economic recovery and the rising tension between the Great Powers. Spain remained under the conservative government of the bienio negro, with increasing polarisation.

Chapter VI — The Middle East

Persia (Iran), Afghanistan, Iraq, Palestine, Syria, and Arabia pursued their separate paths. The Shah of Persia paid a state visit abroad for the first time. Tensions in Palestine between Jewish immigrants and the Arab population continued. Iraq moved towards greater independence from British mandate oversight.

Chapter VII — The Far East

China continued to face Japanese encroachment in Manchuria and north China. Japan consolidated its position in Manchukuo and pushed for greater influence. The Netherlands East Indies remained under Dutch colonial administration.

Chapter VIII — Morocco and Egypt

In Morocco, the French completed their campaign of pacification in the south. Egypt continued to negotiate with Britain over the terms of its independence and the status of the Suez Canal.

Chapter IX — America

The United States, under President Franklin D. Roosevelt, pushed forward with the New Deal. The Reciprocal Trade Agreements Act was passed, and relations with Latin America were shaped by the Good Neighbor Policy. The Chaco War between Bolivia and Paraguay continued. Brazil, Chile, Cuba, Mexico, Peru, and Uruguay each faced domestic challenges.

Part II

Chronicle of Events in 1934

A day-by-day record of notable events throughout the year, from January to December.

Retrospect of Literature, Art, and Science

Literature, Art, Drama, the Cinema, Music, and the Sciences were surveyed. The year saw notable works by H.G. Wells, Winifred Holtby, and others; in science, the discovery of artificial radioactivity by Irène and Frédéric Joliot-Curie was a landmark.

Public Documents

I. The Baltic Pact — Treaty of Agreement and Collaboration between Lithuania, Estonia, and Latvia (September 12, 1934), establishing co-operation among the three Baltic states.

Obituary of Eminent Persons Deceased in 1934

Included: Jakob Wassermann (novelist), Marie Curie (scientist), King Albert I of the Belgians (killed in a climbing accident), Paul von Hindenburg (German President), Engelbert Dollfuss (Austrian Chancellor), Sir Edward Elgar (composer), Sir James Barrie (playwright), and Kurt von Schleicher (killed in the Night of the Long Knives).
