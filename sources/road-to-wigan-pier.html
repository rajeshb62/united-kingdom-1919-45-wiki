The Road to Wigan Pier (1937)

George Orwell’s report from the depressed industrial North of England, combined with a searching examination of English class feeling and of why Socialism — which he treats as the only real way out of the mess — so often repels the very people who ought to support it.

Summary

Commissioned in the mid-1930s and published by Victor Gollancz for the Left Book Club in 1937, The Road to Wigan Pier falls into two clearly marked parts. Part One is a first-hand account of living conditions among miners and the unemployed in Lancashire and Yorkshire: lodging-houses, the coal face, housing shortage, the Means Test and the dole, diet, coal-picking, and the landscape of industrialism. Part Two turns autobiographical and polemical. Orwell situates himself as a product of the lower-upper-middle class, traces the formation of class smell and class prejudice from public school through service in the Burma police to tramping among the destitute, and argues that the failure of Socialism in England is less a matter of economics than of manners, snobbery, crankishness, and the identification of Socialism with a machine-worshipping cult of “progress.” He closes with a call to strip Socialism back to justice and liberty and to win the sinking middle class before Fascism does. The book is a primary witness to the social and political atmosphere of interwar industrial England on the eve of rearmament and war.

Source

Orwell, George. The Road to Wigan Pier. London: Victor Gollancz Ltd, 1937. Issued for the Left Book Club.

Chapters

Part One

Chapter 1

Orwell opens in a crowded Wigan lodging-house run by the Brookers, describing the shared bedroom, the stench, the tripe shop downstairs, commercial travellers, old-age pensioners, and the landlady’s endless grievances. The chapter is a close portrait of petty commercial decay and domestic squalor among people living on the edge of the Means Test, and it ends as Orwell leaves Wigan by train, watching snow and slag-heaps give way to another industrial town and reflecting that even in the heart of industrialism patches of green remain.

Chapter 2

“Our civilization, pace Chesterton, is founded on coal.” Orwell describes going down a mine to the coal face while the fillers are at work: heat, noise, dust, the yard-high gallery, and the exhausting “travelling” to and from the face that is not even counted as the miner’s real work. He treats the miner as the type of the invisible manual worker on whose labour “superior persons” depend, recalling that women once dragged coal underground in harness and arguing that modern comfort rests on work the rest of society prefers to forget.

Chapter 3

Above ground, Orwell watches a shift leave the pit — pale faces under coal dust, blue scars from dust under healed cuts, the ritual of washing after tea — and turns to wages, output, and accident rates. He cites production figures (around 280 tons a year for the average mine-worker) and the “death stoppage” rubber stamp on pay-checks, noting that roughly one miner in nine hundred is killed each year and one in six injured. The chapter closes on the broader indignity of working-class life: waiting on other people’s convenience, the sense that “they” will never allow anything, and the social ease with which the “educated” still step forward as leaders.

Chapter 4

A survey of housing in the industrial towns: smoke-blackened rows without hot water, shared yards and w.c.s, houses unfit for habitation but kept tenanted because there are no others, caravan colonies and single rooms of extreme overcrowding, and Corporation estates that are only marginally better. Orwell records inspections of many houses and the courtesy of unemployed guides, and ends with a personal note on Wigan itself — liking the people, not the scenery — and the joke that the celebrated Wigan Pier has been demolished.

Chapter 5

Orwell attacks the habit of reading “two millions unemployed” as if only those two million suffered. Dependants, low-paid full-time workers, pensioners, and the unregistered push the underfed population far higher (he cites Sir John Orr and multiplies Labour Exchange figures). He describes the Means Test, P.A.C. allowances, occupational centres, and the National Unemployed Workers’ Movement, and argues that cheap luxuries — fish-and-chips, the radio, cinema, football pools, tinned salmon — have mitigated surface misery and helped avert revolution without any conscious “bread and circuses” design by the governing class.

Chapter 6

Food first: Napoleon’s maxim and the claim that diet shapes history more than dynasties. On about thirty shillings a week with rent taking a quarter, the unemployed diet is white bread, margarine, sugar, tea, potatoes, and the occasional scrap of meat — filling but deficient. Orwell links industrial substitutes and tinned food to a decline in physique, describes wasteful English cookery even among the poor, and closes with the Lancashire scene of unemployed men and women risking injury to pick coal and “carmel” from dirt trains for a few pence worth of fuel.

Chapter 7

Travelling north, Orwell marks the passage from southern villa-civilisation into the “real ugliness of industrialism”: burning slag-heaps, sulphur flames, canals, and the lunar landscape around Wigan. He digresses on the English North–South myth (cold climate, Nordic virtue, the cult of Scotland) and on how industrialisation twisted that antithesis. The chapter ends with a working-class interior — coal fire, kippers, strong tea — as a moment in time, contrasting it both with a hygienic Utopian future and with medieval squalor, and treating that interior as evidence that the age has not been altogether a bad one to live in.

Part Two

Chapter 8

“The road from Mandalay to Wigan is a long one.” Orwell explains why he went north — to see mass unemployment and the working class at close quarters as part of his approach to Socialism — and turns to autobiography. Born into the lower-upper-middle class (roughly £300–£2,000 a year, with a caste element as well as money), he analyses English class as a shadowy system of habits and smells, not income alone. He discusses the physical repugnance that divides classes in the West, quotes Somerset Maugham on smell, and exposes the bourgeois Socialist who idealises the proletariat while keeping bourgeois table manners and marrying inside his own class.

Chapter 9

Public-school snobbery, a scholarship boy among richer boys, and the wave of revolutionary feeling in England during and just after the Great War form the opening. Orwell recounts service in the Indian Imperial Police in Burma — the dirty work of Empire at close quarters, guilt shared with another official on a night train, the missionary who would not care to have his job — and his decision to resign. He then describes deliberately going among the destitute: a common lodging-house as “baptism,” tramping with an Irish tramp, and the sense that the class-bar was down among “the lowest of the low” (material already used in Down and Out in Paris and London).

Chapter 10

Making friends with tramps does not solve the class problem: outcasts are no more typical of the working class than literary intellectuals are of the bourgeoisie. Orwell argues that real intimacy with ordinary respectable working people is extremely hard, that abolishing class distinctions means abandoning middle-class ideology rather than attending summer schools or “levelling up” with fruit-juice and hygiene, and that forced class-breaking often intensifies antagonism. The middle-class Socialist who recoils in middle age may flee as far as Fascism once he sees that a classless society might mean the death of his own codes and tastes.

Chapter 11

“Meanwhile what about Socialism?” The country is in a serious mess: insecurity, mass underfeeding, a pinched middle class, and worse horrors possibly coming. Socialism as a world system is elementary common sense — the raft has provisions enough if shared — yet intelligent people often reject it. Orwell catalogues the unattractiveness of many Socialists (cranks, fruit-juice drinkers, Marx-quoting cocksure types) and the barrenness of Socialist literary culture, arguing that ordinary people’s recoil from individual Socialists, however childish, is a political fact that must be faced.

Chapter 12

A deeper objection: people fear not that Socialism will fail, but that it will succeed too well — a spiritually empty, machine-dominated future. Orwell links the word “Socialism” to aeroplanes, tractors, glass factories, and also to vegetarians, commissars, and the cult of Russia; he discusses machine-worship, the alienation of anyone with feeling for tradition, and comparisons of Wells’s The Sleeper Awakes with Huxley’s Brave New World. Fascism he reads as a possible stable slave-world with a small ruling caste. Against both machine-prig Socialism and Fascism he insists the only combinable ideal is justice and liberty — words that must ring again if Fascism is not to win.

Chapter 13

Is there anything one can do? The urgent task is to capture “normal decent” people for Socialism before Fascism plays its trump card. Orwell urges dropping the smell of crankishness and machine-worship, recognising that class is cultural as well as economic (the sinking middle class still thinks in bourgeois terms), and avoiding muscular efforts at instant class-breaking. He hopes that a real Socialist party, if formed in time, may soften class prejudice through common struggle; otherwise he foresees “a slimy Anglicized form of Fascism.” The book ends with the sinking middle class — schoolmasters, journalists, clerks, bankrupt drapers — invited to sink into the working class: “we have nothing to lose but our aitches.”
