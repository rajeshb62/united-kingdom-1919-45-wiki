The Annual Register for 1932

Part I — English History

Chapter I — The General Tariff

The year opened with the supreme question of whether the national accounts would balance by March 31; the Budget of October 1931, balanced on paper, depended heavily on income-tax revenue. The public’s extraordinary response to an appeal for prompt tax payments averted a feared collapse of the currency. The Government introduced its General Tariff, with the Import Duties Bill imposing a general ad valorem duty of 10 per cent. on foreign manufactured goods, marking Britain’s historic break with free trade. The Bill passed after fierce debate, with the Liberal ministers — Sir Herbert Samuel and his colleagues — deeply divided, ultimately adopting an “agreement to differ” that allowed them to remain in the National Government while opposing protection.

Chapter II — The Budget and the Conversion Loan

Parliament resumed after Easter with the Wheat Bill’s passage and continued debates on the means test and transitional payments for the unemployed. On April 19, the Chancellor presented a Budget that increased the standard rate of income tax to 5s. in the pound and raised surtax, while the House debated the new tariff scheme. The great event of the chapter was the launching of the Conversion Loan — the conversion of £2,000,000,000 of 5 per cent. War Loan to 3½ per cent. — which succeeded beyond expectation, with 92 per cent. of holders accepting the terms, saving the Exchequer over £23,000,000 annually. Relations with Ireland deteriorated sharply over the oath and land annuities, with Mr. de Valera’s government withholding payments.

Chapter III — The Ottawa Agreements

The British delegation, led by Mr. Baldwin and Mr. Chamberlain, departed for the Imperial Economic Conference at Ottawa in July. The Conference produced a series of bilateral trade agreements granting帝国 preferences, with Britain agreeing to maintain or impose duties on foreign foodstuffs in exchange for preferential treatment from the Dominions. The agreements split the Cabinet: the Free Trade ministers — Sir Herbert Samuel, Sir Archibald Sinclair, and Lord Snowden — resigned rather than accept the protectionist policy, while Mr. MacDonald remained as Prime Minister of a reconstructed National Government. The Ottawa Agreements Bill passed through Parliament, formally committing Britain to a new direction in imperial trade policy.

Chapter IV — The American Debt Payment

As winter approached, the vast army of unemployed faced deepening hardship, with protests against the means test culminating in the “Hunger March” and outbreaks of violence. The Government introduced supplementary estimates for unemployment and debated relief for farmers hit by the sharp decline in agricultural prices. The chapter centres on the war debt question: with the Hoover Moratorium expiring, Britain made a $95,000,000 payment to the United States — described as a “transfer” rather than a “payment” — after an acrimonious exchange of notes. The payment was made on December 15, but the episode left Anglo-American relations strained and the principle of debt revision unresolved.

Imperial History

Chapter I — Ireland

The outstanding event was the opening of the new Parliament Buildings at Stormont, Belfast, by the Prince of Wales on November 16. Northern Ireland passed several industrial Bills but faced growing nationalist protests; Nationalist members walked out of the House of Commons in May over restrictions on debating Imperial questions. In the Irish Free State, Mr. de Valera’s government pursued its constitutional course, withholding the land annuities and removing the oath of allegiance, deepening the rift with Britain.

Chapter II — Canada

The Bennett Conservative Administration opened its third session in February, with the Governor-General’s Speech emphasising rigid economy despite universal economic disturbance. Canada’s trade balance held favourable, and a National Service Loan was oversubscribed. The Ottawa Conference loomed large in Canadian calculations, with the government seeking secure markets for its wheat, timber, and other primary products. Canada’s departure from the gold standard in the spring created a new currency alignment with sterling.

Chapter III — The Union of South Africa

The Union Government found itself increasingly embarrassed by the economic crisis, with General Smuts warning of the alternatives of “gold bankruptcy or sterling.” The year was dominated by the decision to abandon the gold standard in December, following Australia and New Zealand. To counteract the effects of exchange depreciation, the government increased export subsidies for agricultural products. The debate over gold versus sterling became entangled with racial politics, as moderates of both white races despaired at the intensification of national ambitions amidst general financial distress.

Chapter IV — Australasia

In Australia, a National Coalition under Mr. Lyons took office following the decisive defeat of Mr. Scullin at the December 1931 election, and was responsible for the Commonwealth’s representation at Ottawa. The chapter details Australia’s tariff negotiations with Britain and the resignation of two ministers — Mr. Fenton and Mr. Hawker — over disagreements on trade and expenditure policy. New Zealand faced similar economic pressures, with falling export prices for wool and dairy products forcing stringent economies.

Chapter V — India

Persistence was the keynote of 1932: persistence of the third civil disobedience campaign renewed by Congress at the beginning of the year, and persistence of the Government in pushing forward constitutional reform. Mr. Gandhi was arrested in January under a Bombay Regulation of 1827, and the Congress Working Committee was declared an unlawful association. Despite the unrest, the Round Table Conference process continued, with the Communal Award published in August, granting separate electorates to minorities, and the third session of the Round Table Conference meeting in London in November.

Foreign History

Chapter I — The League of Nations

The year 1932 became the annus terribilis for the League, as the economic blizzard intensified and international trade declined to less than half its 1929 volume. The League’s Disarmament Conference opened in Geneva in February but made little progress, overshadowed by the deepening depression and the rise of nationalist economic policies. The Lytton Commission’s report on the Manchurian crisis was published in October, condemning Japan’s actions but failing to produce effective international action.

Chapter II — France and Italy

France faced the triple problems of reparations, debts, and disarmament. The death of M. Maginot in January led to a Cabinet reconstruction under M. Laval, with M. Tardieu succeeding to the War Ministry. The chapter covers France’s strained budgetary position, the Lausanne Conference on reparations, and the growing political instability. Italy under Mussolini maintained its fascist corporative system, with the economy strained by the world slump and the government’s emphasis on autarky and public works.

Chapter III — Germany and Austria

The most significant feature of Germany’s internal history was the spread of political extremism: Hitler’s National Socialists became by far the largest single party. President von Hindenburg was re-elected in the spring after a bitter contest against Hitler, but a rapid succession of Chancellors — Brüning, von Papen, and von Schleicher — weakened the authority of the State. The July elections gave the Nazis 230 seats, but Hindenburg refused to appoint Hitler as Chancellor. The Lausanne Conference effectively ended German reparations. Austria struggled with the aftermath of the Creditanstalt collapse and growing Nazi agitation.

Chapter IV — Soviet Russia and Eastern Europe

The Soviet Five-Year Plan made indifferent progress: oil, electric, and machine-building industries were ahead of schedule, but coal, steel, and pig-iron were considerably behindhand, and transport remained unsatisfactory. A second Five-Year Plan was launched amidst increasing qualitative defects in production. The chapter covers the economies of Poland, Czechoslovakia, Hungary, Rumania, Yugoslavia, Turkey, Greece, and the Baltic states, all grappling with the depression and the collapse of agricultural prices.

Chapter V — Western and Northern Europe

Financial anxieties weighed heavily on Belgium, whose franc weathered a serious crisis but faced a budget deficit of a milliard francs. The chapter follows the economic difficulties of the Netherlands, Switzerland, Spain (under its new republican constitution), Portugal, and the Scandinavian states — Denmark, Sweden, Norway, and Finland — all of which experienced severe trade contraction and rising unemployment.

Chapter VI — The Middle East

The chapter covers Persia’s frontier rectification with Turkey, the state visit of King Feisal of Iraq to Teheran, ongoing developments in Palestine under the British Mandate (including Jewish immigration and Arab opposition), French administration in Syria, and the consolidation of Ibn Saud’s position in Arabia.

Chapter VII — The Far East

The year was notable for Japan’s deepening involvement in Manchuria following the Mukden Incident of 1931, and the Shanghai hostilities of January–March, in which Japanese forces bombarded the Chinese city. The League’s Lytton Report, while condemning Japan, failed to halt its advance. China experienced somewhat greater political stability under Chiang Kai-shek, who returned to power, but the civilian government remained feeble against military despotism.

Chapter VIII — Morocco and Egypt

French operations in the High Atlas were crowned with success, the Tafilet oases being occupied in January. In Egypt, the Wafd government under Sidky Pasha continued to grapple with economic difficulties, while the question of Anglo-Egyptian relations — particularly the status of the Sudan and the Capitulations — remained unresolved.

Chapter IX — America

The “economic blizzard” continued at hurricane force through the first half of 1932. Wholesale prices fell to their lowest levels since 1908; wheat touched 41½ cents a bushel — below the tariff. Unemployment was estimated at over 12,000,000. The presidential election in November resulted in a landslide victory for Franklin D. Roosevelt over President Hoover. The chapter also covers the Chaco War between Bolivia and Paraguay and the economic difficulties of Argentina, Brazil, Chile, and Mexico.

Part II — Chronicle of Events in 1932

A day-by-day chronicle of notable events throughout the year, covering Britain and the wider world. January opened with the New Year Honours and Princess Mary being granted the title of Princess Royal; the first regular air-mail service between London and Cape Town began on January 20. The year included the opening of the Sydney Harbour Bridge in March, the disappearance of Mr. and Mrs. Mollison in April (they were later found safe), and President von Hindenburg’s re-election in April. The “Hunger March” of the unemployed reached London in October, and November saw Roosevelt’s election victory and the opening of the Ottawa buildings.

Retrospect of Literature, Art, and Science in 1932

Literature: [by J. Isaacs] The year’s notable publications included John Galsworthy winning the Nobel Prize for Literature, Leon Trotsky’s History of the Russian Revolution, and a wide range of historical and biographical works covering Georgian history, Roman Britain, and European social history.

Art: [by W. T. Whitley] The art world saw major exhibitions at the Royal Academy and elsewhere, with continued interest in modern movements as well as traditional British painting.

Drama: [by Ernest Kuhe] The London stage premiered significant new works, with the theatre showing resilience despite economic pressures.

The Cinema: [by Monica Ewer] The film industry continued its expansion, with the advent of talking pictures fully established and British studios producing an increasing number of feature films.

Music: [by Ernest Kuhe] The musical season included notable performances at Covent Garden and the Proms, with both classical and contemporary composers represented.

The Biological Sciences: [by W. B. Brierley] Advances in genetics, biochemistry, and agricultural science were recorded, with continued research into plant and animal breeding.

The Physical Sciences: Key developments in physics and chemistry were noted, building on the foundations of quantum mechanics and nuclear research.

Finance and Commerce in 1932

The world economic crisis became more intense, but exceptional success attended British efforts, and the prestige of Great Britain stood higher than that of any other leading nation at the close of the year. Britain’s departure from the gold standard in September 1931, combined with the new General Tariff, gave British industry an estimated 70 per cent. advantage over gold-standard competitors. The Conversion of the War Loan from 5 to 3½ per cent. saved £23,000,000 annually. The visible adverse balance of trade was drastically reduced, and there was a marked increase in factory building. The chapter covers the Ottawa Agreements’ financial provisions, the Exchange Equalisation Account, and the continuing difficulty of international debt settlement.

Law in 1932

The year saw important reports from the Gregory Committee on Unemployment Insurance (recommending reconstitution on bona fide insurance lines) and the Rent Restriction Committee. The Committee on Departmental Powers reviewed the delegation of legislative authority, recommending a Standing Committee of the House of Commons to scrutinise such delegations. In criminal law, the Persistent Offenders Committee recommended prolonged detention for habitual criminals. Notable legal cases included the protracted disputes arising from the means test administration and the constitutional questions surrounding the “agreement to differ” in the National Government.

Public Documents

Three major documents are reproduced: (I) The Imperial Economic Conference at Ottawa — Summary of Conclusions, covering the bilateral trade agreements, Empire content requirements, and monetary policy resolutions; (II) The British Note to the United States Government relating to the War Debt payment of December 1932, setting out Britain’s position that the payment was a “transfer” made under protest; (III) The Non-Aggression Pact between the U.S.S.R. and France, signed in November 1932.

Obituary of Eminent Persons Deceased in 1932

Notable figures who died in 1932 included: Aristide Briand, the former French Premier and Nobel Peace Prize winner; Albert, King of the Belgians (in a climbing accident on February 17); M. Maginot, the French Minister of War; Lord Balfour, the British statesman and author of the Balfour Declaration; M. Doumer, President of France (assassinated); John Galsworthy, the novelist and Nobel laureate; Henry George, the American economist; and Sir William Osler, the renowned physician.
