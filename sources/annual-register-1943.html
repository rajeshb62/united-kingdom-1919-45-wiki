The Annual Register for 1943

Part I — English History

Chapter I — The Casablanca Conference

When the year 1943 opened the British Eighth Army under General Montgomery was at Wadi el Kebir, two hundred miles east of Tripoli, pursuing Rommel’s Afrika Korps. By January 23 Tripoli was taken; the Casablanca Conference in mid-January produced the doctrine of unconditional surrender and co-ordinated Allied strategy for the Mediterranean. The chapter covers the Eighth Army’s advance into Tunisia, the political situation in North Africa, Chinese extra-territoriality renounced, the Beveridge Report debate, compulsory arbitration in mining, and the establishment of the Ministry of Town and Country Planning.

Chapter II — The Conquest of Tunisia and Sicily

The Budget for 1943-44 introduced pay-as-you-earn income tax and addressed external costs and price control. By May the Axis surrendered in Tunisia, yielding nearly 250,000 prisoners. In July the Allies invaded Sicily; Messina fell in August, opening the straits and securing the central Mediterranean. Churchill visited Washington. The chapter also covers the Bermuda Conference on refugees, the new Planning Bill, civil aviation policy, and the Conservative Party Conference.

Chapter III — The Invasion of Italy

The capture of Catania on August 5 opened new possibilities in the eastern Mediterranean. The Quebec Conference (Quadrant) in August set strategy for the Italian campaign and the cross-Channel invasion. Italy surrendered on September 3, the Badoglio government declared war on Germany, and Allied forces landed at Salerno. The Italian fleet surrendered at Malta. The chapter covers the TUC conference, trade union relations with the government, and the King’s Speech opening the new session.

Chapter IV — The Moscow, Cairo, and Teheran Conferences

In October General Smuts came to London for War Cabinet consultations, speaking of victory being in sight. The Moscow Conference of Foreign Ministers (Molotov, Eden, Hull) in October issued a declaration on post-war security and established the European Advisory Council. At Cairo and Teheran in November-December, Churchill, Roosevelt, and Stalin met for the first time, agreeing on the cross-Channel invasion (Overlord), the partition of Germany, and the post-war treatment of Poland.

The Empire

Chapter I — Ireland

In Northern Ireland a change of premiership brought Sir Basil Brooke to office in place of J. M. Andrews, forming a new Cabinet.

Chapter II — Canada

The year was one of historic achievement in Canada’s total war effort, marking the Dominion’s growing status as a world power, though political developments in Ontario and Quebec showed a trend towards lower taxation and resistance to conscription.

Chapter III — South Africa

Field-Marshal Smuts dominated the year with important utterances on the war and post-war world. He was returned with a decisive majority in the July general election. The chapter also covers Southern Rhodesia.

Chapter IV — Australasia

Australia’s Labour government, with equal Government and Opposition strength in the House at the start of the year, consolidated its position. New Zealand continued its war contribution alongside Australian forces in the Pacific theatre.

Chapter V — India and Burma

Neither the flowing tide of war in favour of the United Nations nor the arrival of the new Viceroy, Lord Wavell, brought any marked change in the obstinate Indian political situation. The policy of non-co-operation remained unrenounced; Burma remained under Japanese occupation.

Foreign History

Chapter I — The League of Nations

Against a background of conferences under United Nations auspices, the League continued to survive and render services, particularly in economic and social fields, refugee work, and the maintenance of international law.

Chapter II — France and Italy

French resistance to German occupation grew substantially. The Committee of National Liberation under General de Gaulle was recognised by the Allies. In Italy the fall of Mussolini, the Badoglio armistice, and the declaration of war on Germany marked the year.

Chapter III — Germany

For Germany 1943 was a black year. Unparalleled defeats began with the disaster at Stalingrad. German armies were driven from Africa with shame. At home conditions for the civilian population deteriorated badly as Allied bombing intensified.

Chapter IV — Soviet Union and Eastern Europe

Nineteen forty-three was a significant year on the Russian Front. The Stalingrad victory was followed by the liberation of the Caucasus, the great tank battle at Kursk, the crossing of the Dnieper, and the recapture of Kiev. Poland, Czechoslovakia, Hungary, Rumania, Yugoslavia, Turkey, Greece, Bulgaria, and Albania each experienced the shifting tides of war and diplomacy.

Chapter V — Lesser States of Western and Northern Europe

Belgium remained under German occupation with intensified resistance. The Netherlands, Switzerland, Spain, Portugal, Denmark, Sweden, Norway, and Finland each navigated the pressures of war, occupation, blockade, and neutrality.

Chapter VI — The Middle East and North Africa

Egypt saw conversations on post-war independence. Palestine and Transjordan experienced continued tension. The Lebanon and Syria moved towards greater self-government. Iraq, Arabia, and the Arab Federation question occupied diplomatic attention. Persia and Morocco faced strategic pressures.

Chapter VII — The Far East

The military situation in China underwent little change; the initiative remained with the Japanese. China’s international position was strengthened by the renunciation of extra-territorial rights. Japan consolidated its occupied territories.

Chapter VIII — America

The United States accelerated war production towards the arsenal of democracy. A press interview by Mr. Wendell Willkie in December 1942 shaped early 1943’s political debates. Argentina, Bolivia, Brazil, Chile, Mexico, and Uruguay each dealt with the pressures of hemispheric solidarity, Axis influence, and alignment with the United Nations.
