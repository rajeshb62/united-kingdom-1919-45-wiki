Hankey, Man of Secrets, Vol. III: 1931 1963

Hankey, Man of Secrets, Vol. III: 1931-1963

Jul 10, 20267 min read

Summary

This third and final volume of Stephen Roskill’s biography of Maurice Hankey covers the period from 1931 to 1963, tracing Hankey’s career from his final years as Cabinet Secretary through the rearmament debates, his Empire tour, the Abyssinia crisis, his elevation to the peerage and appointment as Minister without Portfolio in Chamberlain’s war cabinet, his dismissal by Churchill in 1942, and his post-war years as a critic of government policy and elder statesman. The volume draws extensively on Hankey’s private papers and official documents to illuminate the inner workings of British defence policy and high-level decision-making across three decades of war and crisis.

Source

Roskill, Stephen Wentworth. Hankey, Man of Secrets. Vol. III: 1931-1963. London: Collins, 1974. ISBN 0 00 211332 5.

Chapters

Foreword and Acknowledgments (p. 1)

Roskill acknowledges the individuals and institutions who assisted with the research and preparation of the third and final volume, including Hankey’s family and the custodians of the relevant archives.

1. First Steps to Recovery. September 1931–December 1932 (p. 21)

Covers the immediate aftermath of the 1931 financial crisis and the formation of the National Government. Hankey continues as Cabinet Secretary, managing the machinery of government through a period of severe economic retrenchment while beginning to confront the emerging strategic challenges posed by Japan’s invasion of Manchuria and the growing threat from Germany.

2. The Fight for Rearmament. January 1933–August 1934 (p. 67)

Deals with Hankey’s central role in the rearmament debate following Hitler’s accession to power in Germany. As Secretary to the Committee of Imperial Defence (CID), Hankey works behind the scenes to build the case for British rearmament, navigating the tensions between the Treasury’s desire for economy and the service departments’ urgent needs, while the Defence Requirements Committee prepares its first report.

3. Empire Tour. August 1934–January 1937 (p. 121)

Covers Hankey’s extensive journey through the Empire — including Australia, New Zealand, and other dominions — to consult on defence coordination. This chapter details his observations on imperial defence problems, his meetings with dominion leaders, and the strategic conclusions he drew about the Empire’s capacity to support Britain in a future war.

4. The Darkening Scene. January–June 1935 (p. 148)

Describes the worsening international situation as Germany openly repudiates the Versailles Treaty and begins conscription, while Italy prepares for war against Abyssinia. Hankey’s growing influence on defence policy is evident as he works to strengthen the CID’s machinery and prepare Britain for the coming crisis.

5. Crisis with Italy. Hankey under Fire. July 1935–February 1936 (p. 176)

Covers the Abyssinian crisis and the imposition of League of Nations sanctions against Italy. Hankey comes under political fire as the Cabinet’s handling of the crisis is criticized. He is deeply involved in the strategic implications for British naval power in the Mediterranean and the damaging effect of the crisis on collective security and Anglo-Italian relations.

6. Germany, Italy and Japan. March–December 1936 (p. 220)

Examines the growing alignment between the three revisionist powers following the Abyssinian crisis. Hankey watches with alarm as Germany remilitarises the Rhineland, Italy draws closer to Germany, and Japan expands in the Far East. The chapter analyses the CID’s response to the multiplication of potential enemies.

7. The Triple Threat Grows. January–December 1937 (p. 255)

Continues the analysis of the three-power threat as the Axis takes shape. Hankey’s workload intensifies as rearmament finally accelerates under a new Defence Loans programme. The chapter covers the Imperial Conference of 1937 and Hankey’s efforts to coordinate dominion defence contributions.

8. ’… for on his choice depends / The safety and the health of the whole state.’ (p. 297)

The chapter title, taken from Kipling’s poem “The Chooser,” reflects the critical turning point as Hankey contemplates retirement from the post of Cabinet Secretary. This chapter covers the transition period as Hankey prepares to leave the office he helped to create and to which he had devoted nearly three decades of his career.

9. Interlude with Ellerman. 1937–1941 (p. 327)

Covers Hankey’s business activities during and after his retirement from the civil service, particularly his directorship with the Ellerman shipping lines. This interlude provides insight into Hankey’s private financial affairs and his continued interest in national affairs from outside Whitehall.

10. Retirement and a New Career. 1938 (p. 352)

Describes Hankey’s formal retirement as Cabinet Secretary and CID Secretary in 1938, his elevation to the peerage as Baron Hankey of The Chart, and his assumption of new public roles. The chapter charts his transition from civil servant to public figure and his continued involvement in defence debates during the Munich crisis.

11. The Last Months of Peace. August 1938–September 1939 (p. 377)

Covers the final year before the outbreak of the Second World War, including the Munich Agreement, the German occupation of Prague, and the frantic diplomacy of the summer of 1939. Hankey, now outside government, observes the slide to war with acute anxiety and offers his counsel to former colleagues.

12. In the War Cabinet and After. 3rd September 1939–10th May 1940 (p. 421)

Covers Hankey’s return to government service as Minister without Portfolio in Chamberlain’s War Cabinet at the outbreak of war. He takes on responsibilities for a range of home-front and economic-warfare matters, including petrol rationing, the Ministry of Information, and coordination of the war effort, serving through the period of the “Phoney War.”

13. Minister outside the War Cabinet. May 1940–July 1941 (p. 467)

Following Churchill’s appointment as Prime Minister in May 1940, Hankey is excluded from the five-member War Cabinet but continues to serve as a minister — first as Chancellor of the Duchy of Lancaster and later as Paymaster-General. This chapter covers his marginalisation from the inner circle of decision-making during the darkest period of the war.

14. Another Step Down. July–December 1941 (p. 497)

Describes Hankey’s further relegation to less important ministerial posts as Churchill consolidates his control over the war machine. Hankey’s influence wanes and his relationship with Churchill becomes increasingly strained, though he continues to work on a range of committee assignments.

15. Dismissal. January–July 1942 (p. 539)

Covers Hankey’s final break with Churchill and his dismissal from the government in the spring of 1942. The chapter analyses the reasons for Churchill’s decision — rooted in policy disagreements, personality clashes, and Churchill’s desire for a leaner administration — and Hankey’s bitter reaction to his exclusion.

16. Constructive Critic. August 1942–August 1945 (p. 562)

After his dismissal, Hankey becomes a vocal critic of government policy from the backbenches and through the press. He focuses on shipping policy, the convoy system, post-war planning, and the conduct of the war, maintaining a stream of memoranda and proposals to those still in power. The chapter covers his efforts to remain relevant until the war’s end.

17. The Last Phase. 1945–1963 (p. 601)

Covers Hankey’s final years, from the immediate post-war period to his death in 1963. He remains active as a writer, commentator, and elder statesman, publishing his memoirs and offering his perspective on the Cold War, the decline of the British Empire, and the nuclear deterrent. The chapter concludes with an assessment of Hankey’s place in British history.

Appendix A: British Defence Services. Annual Estimates 1929–1939 (p. 663)

Tabulated data on British defence expenditure across the three services in the decade before the Second World War.

Appendix B: German Air Strength 1934–1939

Data on the growth of the German Luftwaffe in the pre-war period.
