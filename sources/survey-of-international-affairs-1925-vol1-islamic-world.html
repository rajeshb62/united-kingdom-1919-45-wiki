Survey of International Affairs, 1925, Volume I — The Islamic World since the Peace Settlement (Toynbee, 1927)

The first of two volumes of the Royal Institute of International Affairs Survey for 1925: Toynbee’s connected history of Islamic countries from the years immediately after the General War through 1925–26 — Caliphate and secularization, North-West Africa and the Rif, Egypt and the Sudan, the Wahhabi conquest of the Hijaz, the French and British mandates, and the Mosul controversy — issued under Chatham House auspices in 1927.

Companions in this wiki: Survey 1926, Survey 1927, Survey 1931, Survey 1936, The World in March 1939. This file is Volume I only. Volume II of the 1925 Survey (Locarno, the American continent, the Far East after Washington), entrusted to other writers when Toynbee could not complete it, is not in this scan.

Summary

G. M. Gathorne-Hardy’s preface explains the exceptional two-part year: Islamic affairs had been crowded out of earlier Surveys and of The History of the Peace Conference; the Committee therefore gave the whole of Volume I to one connected Islamic treatment covering not only 1925 but the post-war years those volumes had skipped. Toynbee’s own introduction treats the post-1918 Islamic movement as part of a world-wide double impulse among non-Western peoples: to throw off Western ascendancy, and at the same time to adopt Western military, political, economic, and cultural technique by choice. The volume then walks Caliphate and secularization; the Maghrib (Libya, Spanish and French Morocco, ‘Abdu’l-Karim, Tangier, Tunisia, Algeria); Egypt, the Sudan, and the Nile; Ibn Sa’ud and the fall of the Hashimids; the Syria and Palestine mandates (including Leonard Stein on the Jewish National Home); Alexandretta and the Iraqi–Syrian and Mosul frontiers; Persia; India, Afghanistan, and the frontier tribes. Eight documentary appendices and four maps close the book.

Contents

Printed pagination as in the 1927 contents (pp. 1–589 body + maps at end).

Preface (G. M. Gathorne-Hardy) and writer’s notes

Islamic affairs omitted from the first Survey and from 1924 are here treated as one subject, including the immediate post-war years outside The History of the Peace Conference. Material overflowed a 500-page mixed volume, so 1925 appears in two parts. Volume II (promised summer 1927) is to cover security and disarmament (Locarno), the American continent, and the Far East and Pacific from the close of the Washington Conference to the end of 1925. Transliteration note (Colonial Office Iraq vs Foreign Office forms; thirty-six English spellings of the Prophet’s name).

PART I — GENERAL

(i) Introduction (pp. 1–24)

Post-war Islam shares a world-wide anti-Western and Westernizing movement. Militancy brings dictators: Mustafa Kemal, Riza Shah Pahlawi, Muhammad ‘Abdu’l-Karim of the Rif, Sultanu’l-Atrash in the Jabal Druze, ‘Abdu’l-‘Aziz b. Sa’ud, Amanullah of Afghanistan. Intellectual nationalists (Zaghlul, Shahbandar, the Ali brothers) fare less well than these soldiers; old-school “Herodians” hold office only behind Western bayonets. Dynasties fall: Ottoman Sultanate 1922, Caliphate 1924, Qajars 1925, Hashimids in the Hijaz 1925; Faysal and Abdullah survive under the British mandate; Fu’ad and the Sharifian Sultan under occupation or Lyautey’s cordon. With the House of Osman go Shari’ah organs, the millet system, and the Capitulations. Turkish secularization and Wahhabi iconoclasm; Indian Khilafat politics shifting from Ottoman to Najdi rallying-point; Shi’i mujtahids block a Persian republic but not Riza’s crown.

(ii) Abolition of the Ottoman Caliphate and secularization (pp. 25–91)

(a) Antecedents of the Ottoman Caliphate and the Sunni–Shi’i schism from ‘Ali. (b) The Hamidian revival, 1876–1922. (c) From the jihad of 23 November 1914 to the law of 1 November 1922 in the Great National Assembly. (d) From the election of ‘Abdu’l-Mejid (18 November 1922) to abolition under the law of 3 March 1924. (e) The secularization movement. (f) The Caliphate Congress (Mu’tamaru’l-Khilafah) at Cairo, 13–19 May 1926.

PART II — NORTH-WEST AFRICA (1920–6) (pp. 92–188)

Maghrib as stepping-stone: Phoenician, Roman, Arab; corsairs until the French descent on Algeria 1830. Reaction against Western ascendancy; genesis of French, Spanish, and Italian titles. Italian Libya and the Sanusiyah; no concerted Maghrib action. Reaction against Spain in Morocco 1909–25; repercussions on the French zone 1924–5; Franco-Spanish co-operation against ‘Abdu’l-Karim (1925); his collapse (1926). Status of Tangier. Nationalism and reform in Tunisia and Algeria 1918–25. Italian settlers in Tunisia. Libyan–Egyptian frontier (Anglo-Italian agreement 31 July 1916 and after).

PART III — THE MIDDLE EAST (pp. 189–570)

Egypt, Sudan, Nile, Egypt–Germany

(i) Egypt and Great Britain (1922–6) — from the Declaration of 28 February 1922 through constitution, elections, the Stack murder trials, and the 1924–26 ministries; note on constitutional developments to 31 December 1925. (ii) Status of the Anglo-Egyptian Sudan (1922–5). (iii) Allocation of the Nile waters. (iv) Egypto-German treaty of 16 June 1925.

Arabia

(v) Resurrection of Wahhabi power and the fall of the House of Hashim — wartime gold and rifles among Arab chiefs; antecedents and course of the Najdi–Hijazi war 1924–5; Ibn Sa’ud proclaimed King of the Hijaz and the Islamic Congress at Mecca (1926); southern Arabia 1919–25. (vi) Frontiers of Ibn Sa’ud with Kuwayt, ‘Iraq, and Transjordan (1921–5).

Mandates

(vii) French mandate for Syria and the Lebanon and British mandate for Palestine. Organization of the mandated territories 1920–4. Leonard Stein on the Jewish National Home, 30 October 1918 – 30 September 1926. Opposition to the mandates 1920–4. France and the Jabal Druze 1920–4. Insurrection in the French mandated territory (1925). High Commissionership of Henry de Jouvenel and the Permanent Mandates Commission. High Commissioners named: Samuel then Plumer; Picot, Gouraud, Weygand, then Sarrail.

(viii) Sanjaq of Alexandretta and the Syro-Turkish frontier from the Franklin-Bouillon Agreement (Angora, 20 October 1921) to the de Jouvenel Agreement (30 May 1926). (ix) ‘Iraq–Syria frontier.

‘Iraq, Mosul, Persia, India–Afghanistan

(x) Ratification of the Anglo-‘Iraqi treaty of 10 October 1922 and the League Council decision of 27 September 1924 on Article 22. (xi) Mosul — characteristics of the vilayet; events from the Armistice of 30 October 1918 to Britain’s appeal of 6 August 1924; diplomacy from the National Pact of 28 January 1920; League commission report 16 July 1925; Kurdish revolt in Turkey February–April 1925; Council award 16 December 1925; Russo-Turkish treaty 17 December 1925; Anglo-‘Iraqi treaty 13 January 1926; Anglo-‘Iraqi–Turkish treaty 5 June 1926; note on oil. (xii) Tension between ‘Iraq and Persia over the exodus of Persian Shi’i ‘ulama from Najaf (return 22 April 1924). (xiii) Persia 1922–5, from the coup of 21 February 1921. (xiv) India, Afghanistan, and the frontier tribes.

Appendices (pp. 571–587)

I. Agha Khan and Ameer Ali to Ismet Pasha, 24 November 1923. II. Three Turkish laws of 3 March 1340 (1924). III. Al-Azhar decision of 25 March 1924 on an Islamic congress. IV. Memorandum of the Third Committee of the Cairo Caliphate Congress, May 1926. V. Muhammad b. ‘Abdi’l-Karimi’l-Khattabi to the Editor of The Times, 27 January 1926. VI. Educational development in Egypt before and after 28 February 1922. VII. Najdi–‘Asiri agreement, 21 October 1926. VIII. Treaty of amity and commerce, San’a, 2 September 1926, Italy and the Yaman.

Index pp. 589–. Maps at end: Northern Morocco; North and Central Arabia; Vilayet of Mosul; the Islamic World at 31 December 1925.
