The Cabinet Office to 1945

A structural and procedural history of the Cabinet Office from its origins in the Committee of Imperial Defence before 1914 through its formal creation in December 1916 to the end of the Churchill administration in July 1945, with a guide to the classification of its surviving records in the Public Record Office. Written by S.S. Wilson, Keeper of Public Records 1960–1966 and later administrative head of the Cabinet Office Historical Section, as a companion to the PRO Handbooks series on Cabinet and CID papers.

Chapter 1 — Introduction

Sets out the constitutional context: the development of Cabinet government from feudal monarchy through to the modern system of collective ministerial responsibility. Explains the creation of the Cabinet Office in December 1916 as the secretarial equipment necessary to record and notify the decisions of Lloyd George’s small War Cabinet, drawing on the existing secretariat of the Committee of Imperial Defence. Describes the nature and value of the Cabinet records as the most valuable single collection of modern official source material for British political history, while noting their limitations (created for current administration, not for historians).

Chapter 2 — The General Procedures of the Cabinet Office 1917–1945

Outlines the standard operating procedures that governed the Office throughout its first three decades: the Instructions to the Secretary (first circulated by Hankey in January 1917 and periodically revised); the circulation of memoranda in locked boxes; the preparation of the agenda by the Prime Minister through the secretary; the conduct of meetings (timing, chairmanship, attendance); the recording of Conclusions (replacing the earlier fuller Minutes from August 1919); handling of dissent; confidential annexes for matters of special secrecy; the weekly report to the Sovereign; the committee system; security arrangements; document symbols and referencing; registered files; the arrangement of Cabinet records in the PRO; staff composition; and the physical location of the Office in 2 Whitehall Gardens and later Great George Street.

Chapter 3 — The Cabinet and Committee of Imperial Defence before 1917

Traces the pre-history of Cabinet procedure from the eighteenth century, when the Cabinet was an informal body of the Sovereign’s principal ministers meeting without agenda or secretary. Shows how the Prime Minister’s letters to the Sovereign served as the sole record. Examines the misunderstandings and constitutional frictions that arose from the lack of formal procedure, the emergence of defence questions as a distinct category requiring systematic attention, the Esher Report of 1904 and the creation of the Committee of Imperial Defence, and Maurice Hankey’s appointment as secretary of the CID in 1912, which built the secretarial staff that would become the nucleus of the Cabinet Office in 1916.

Chapter 4 — The War Cabinet 1916–1919 and the Lloyd George Administration to October 1922

Covers the creation of Lloyd George’s five-member War Cabinet in December 1916 and the rapid establishment of its secretariat, including the ‘Garden Suburb’ of the Prime Minister’s personal advisers. Describes the new ministries created in 1916–17 (Shipping, Labour, Food, Pensions, National Service, Air). Examines the proliferation of memoranda (the GT and early CP series), the elaborate committee structure (War Policy, War Priorities, Eastern Committee, Economic Defence and Development, Home Affairs, Demobilisation), the Imperial War Cabinet and War Conference, the Peace Conference of 1919, the transition to a peace-time Cabinet of twenty members in November 1919, the Haldane Report on the Machinery of Government, and the growing political criticism that culminated in the Conservative election pledge to abolish the Cabinet Secretariat in 1922.

Chapter 5 — The Cabinet November 1922–September 1939

Examines the survival of the Cabinet Office after the Conservative victory of 1922, when Bonar Law’s government ‘provisionally’ approved the Instructions to the Secretary despite the party’s election pledge to abolish the secretariat. Follows the Office through the interwar period: Warren Fisher’s Treasury minute on the propriety of the secretariat, the establishment of the five-day rule for circulation of memoranda, the introduction of press communiqués in 1924, leakage of information, the custody of and access to Cabinet papers, the evolution of the 50-year and later 30-year rules for public access, the growth of the Cabinet committee system, subjects considered in Cabinet, political landmarks of the period, staff changes, and Hankey’s retirement in 1938 with Edward Bridges as his successor. Assesses Hankey’s overall contribution.

Chapter 6 — The Committee of Imperial Defence 1918–1939

Describes the revival of the CID after the First World War, initially through a Standing Defence Sub-Committee in 1920–23, which discussed the Singapore base, the Washington Conference and the future of the RAF. Covers the CID’s monthly meetings through the 1920s and their decline in the early 1930s before a significant increase after 1935. Examines the CID’s work on the ‘ten-year assumption’ (the rule that no major war would occur within ten years), the organisation for defence planning, grand strategy, disarmament policy, the rearmament programme after 1935, the creation of the Minister for Co-ordination of Defence (Inskip, later Chatfield) in 1936, and the CID’s relations with the Dominions.

Chapter 7 — The Committee of Civil Research and the Economic Advisory Council

Covers the post-Haldane Report attempt to extend the CID model into civil policy. Traces the establishment of the Committee of Civil Research under Balfour in 1925, its investigation into the iron and steel industry and other economic questions through a score of expert sub-committees, and its gradual decline. Describes its replacement under MacDonald by the Economic Advisory Council in 1930, the work of the Committee of Economists (including Keynes), and the later standing committees on Scientific Research and on Economic Information that reported to the Cabinet during the rearmament period. Also covers the Cabinet Committee on Non-Intervention in Spain.

Chapter 8 — Imperial and International Conferences 1915–1939

Provides a comprehensive chronological list of the imperial and international conferences for which the Cabinet Office provided the secretariat for the United Kingdom delegation from 1915 to 1939. These include the Anglo-French and Allied conferences and Supreme War Council meetings of 1917–18, the Imperial War Cabinets and Conferences, the Peace Conference, the Washington Disarmament Conference (1921–22), the Genoa Conference (1922), the Imperial Conferences of 1923, 1926, 1930 and 1937, the London Naval Conference (1930), the Ottawa Imperial Economic Conference (1932), the Lausanne Conference (1932), and many others.

Chapter 9 — The War Cabinet 1939–1945

The longest and most detailed chapter, covering the supreme control machinery of the Second World War. Begins with Hankey’s 1928 and 1938 papers to the CID on the options for supreme control. Describes Chamberlain’s nine-member War Cabinet of September 1939, Churchill’s five-member coalition War Cabinet of May 1940, and the Caretaker Cabinet of May–July 1945. Examines general procedure (agenda, circulation of memoranda, meetings, minutes, conclusions and confidential annexes), the contrast between Chamberlain’s orderly administration and Churchill’s more erratic methods, ministerial appointments overseas, the elaborate committee structure (Defence Committee (Operations), Defence Committee (Supply), the Chiefs of Staff Committee and its 2,000+ meetings, the Military Co-ordination Committee, the Combined Chiefs of Staff, the Battle of the Atlantic Committee, Night Air Defence, the Tank Parliament, the Lord President’s Committee, the Import Executive, the Production Executive, the Manpower Committee, the Economic Section, the Central Statistical Office, the Ministry of Reconstruction). Covers international meetings, staff complement, and closes with an assessment of Churchill’s impact and the Office’s achievement.

Chapter 10 — The Historical Section

Traces the origins of the Historical Section to Esher’s 1906 recommendation for a department to compile naval and military history under the CID, the completion of the history of the South African War and the Russo-Japanese War, and the decision of 1914 that the section should focus on wars involving Britain. Describes its transfer to the aegis of the Cabinet Office and its main work: the Official Histories of military operations in the First World War (the term ‘Official’ was dropped in the Second World War to avoid misunderstanding about the status of the accounts), and the civil and military histories series for the Second World War. Notes that authors were given free access to official documents and facilities for consulting participants, while respecting constitutional conventions on collective responsibility and civil service anonymity.

Annexes

The volume includes fourteen annexes providing documentary and statistical material:

  1. Recollections of Cabinet Procedure before 1917 — Sir Herbert Creedy’s 1949 memoir of pre-war practice
  2. The War Cabinet: Rules of Procedure, January 1917 — Hankey’s original rules of procedure
  3. Cabinet Procedure, April 1936 — The codified instructions to the Secretary circulated as CP 88(36), with appendices covering Cabinet committees, CID procedure, and the Economic Advisory Council, plus the Treasury minute establishing the Committee of Civil Research
  4. Notes on War Cabinet Procedure, May 1943 — The procedural rules for Churchill’s War Cabinet
  5. Staff List, 1 January 1945 — Full establishment of the War Cabinet Office
  6. List of Administrative Staff 1916–1945 — Names and tenures of senior staff
  7. Notes to Tables in Annexes 7(a), 7(b), 8 and 9 — Explanatory notes
  8. Meetings of Cabinet, CID and Committees, and Statistics of Staff 1916–1939 — Statistical tables
  9. Meetings of War Cabinet and Committees, and Statistics of Staff 1939–1945 — Statistical tables
  10. Ministers Originating Cabinet Business 1923–1945 — Analysis of papers by originating minister
  11. Ministers Originating Papers to the Lord President’s Committee 1940–1945
  12. Histories Sponsored by the Cabinet Office — List of published official histories
  13. List of Committees (in three parts: before 1923, 1923–1939, 1939–1945)
  14. List of Cabinet Office Posts — Classification and salary scales
