The Annual Register for 1944

Part I — English History

Chapter I — The Education Bill

At the opening of 1944 preparations were being actively made for the invasion of the Continent. The Teheran Conference had proclaimed the undertaking approaching; Eisenhower, Montgomery, and Tedder transferred headquarters from North Africa to Great Britain. The chapter covers the British air offensive, the Anzio landing and its disappointing results, the fight at Cassino, the Education Bill’s second reading and committee stage with the Government defeat and subsequent concessions, by-election surprises, the Reinstatement in Civil Employment Bill, Lord Beaverbrook on civil aviation, the British contribution to UNRRA, the National Health Service White Paper, the Royal Commission on Population, increased bombing of Germany, and the reorganisation of the Army.

Chapter II — The Invasion of Normandy

The invasion of the Continent had been timed for the beginning of June. The Budget for 1944-45 introduced the White Paper on Monetary Policy with Lord Keynes’s defence, the White Paper on Full Employment, electoral reform, and the coal industry agreement. The chapter covers the Wolfram agreements with Spain and Portugal, the Fleming Report on public schools, the Town and Country Planning Bill, D-Day (June 6), the capture of Bayeux, the fighting round Caen, the American capture of Cherbourg, the commencement of flying-bomb raids on London and the exodus from the capital, and the progress in Italy with the capture of Rome on June 4.

Chapter III — The Liberation of France and Belgium

The American breakout at Avranches proved the turning-point in the battle of Normandy. The Canadian offensive towards Falaise destroyed the German Seventh Army. A second Allied landing in Southern France followed. Belgium was liberated, Brussels and Antwerp fell to the British. The flying-bomb assault on London ended, though rocket bombs (V-2) began. The chapter covers the airborne attack on Holland, the British advance to Nijmegen, the attack on Arnhem, the securing of the Nijmegen salient, the clearing of the Scheldt estuary, the Quebec Conference (Octagon), the change in European political alignments, and the TUC congress.

Chapter IV — The Reconstruction Programme

The opening of the port of Antwerp heralded final victory in the near future. The chapter covers the expectation of a general election, Churchill’s Moscow visit in October, recognition of the Provisional French Government, the Dumbarton Oaks proposals for a new international organisation, the new Social Insurance scheme, the demobilisation plans, the redistribution of seats, the housing crisis and temporary housing, the Greek crisis and the clash between British troops and E.A.M., the Labour Party conference’s decision to remain in the Coalition, the Polish question, the disbandment of the Home Guard, and the fixing of agricultural prices.

The Empire

Chapter I — Ireland

In Northern Ireland the issue of a White Paper on Education was the most important event in a politically uneasy year. A measure to restore powers to Belfast Corporation caused internal differences. The suspension of travel between Great Britain and Eire continued.

Chapter II — Canada

Canada’s national war effort was vigorously maintained throughout the year. Political divisions from the continuous strain on man-power and economic conditions became more marked. Provincial general elections revealed divergent trends, and the Federal Government faced a Cabinet crisis at the close of the year.

Chapter III — South Africa and Southern Rhodesia

The year revealed more clearly the importance of South Africa’s productive contributions to the war effort. The Union astonished itself at its economic adaptability and five years of achievement, looking forward with confidence to solving post-war problems and contributing to the establishment of a stable world order.

Chapter IV — Australasia

A conference at Canberra of Australia and New Zealand representatives discussed problems of mutual interest arising from the war. The Australian government under Curtin consolidated its position. New Zealand continued its war contribution alongside Australian forces in the Pacific.

Chapter V — India and Burma

India’s contribution to the Allied war effort was brought more clearly to view by the exploits of her soldiers in Western theatres and the change on her Eastern flank from the defensive to the offensive. The year saw the enemy driven from her frontiers and from great parts of Northern and Central Burma. The Cripps offer’s rejection left the political question unresolved.

Foreign History

Chapter I — The League of Nations

More noticeably than in any previous war year, the League carried out its work in a disturbing atmosphere of uncertainty regarding its future status. Forty-five sovereign States maintained membership. The organisation continued to discharge duties connected with international co-operation while members discussed post-war international organisation elsewhere.

Chapter II — France and Italy

In France further changes made the Vichy Government a more reliable instrument of Nazi policy. Philippe Henriot replaced Paul Marion, presenting the Vichy case with force. General de Gaulle’s Committee of National Liberation gained recognition. In Italy, the Allied advance continued through the year, with the fall of Rome in June followed by further progress northward.

Chapter III — Germany

Four outstanding events marked the year for Germany: decisive military defeats in the East and the West, the attempt on Hitler’s life (July 20), the domination of the Army by the Nazi Party, and the slow but sure disintegration of the home front. The liberation of Italy and France and the advance of the Red Army towards German soil forced Germany onto the defensive.

Chapter IV — Soviet Russia and Eastern Europe

At the beginning of the year the failure of the Western Powers to open a second front caused irritation in Russia. Leningrad was finally relieved in January. The Red Army advanced across the Dnieper into Poland, reaching the Vistula by August and the frontiers of East Prussia. The Warsaw uprising and its destruction, the Baltic States, Poland, Czechoslovakia, Hungary, Rumania, Yugoslavia (Tito’s partisans), Turkey, Greece (the civil conflict), Bulgaria, and Albania each experienced the shifting tides of war and diplomacy.

Chapter V — Lesser States of Western and Northern Europe

For three-quarters of the year Belgium continued to experience the miseries of Nazi occupation — loss of liberty, forced labour, deportations, and mass arrests. The Netherlands endured the Hongerwinter. Switzerland, Spain, Portugal, Denmark, Iceland, Sweden, Norway, and Finland each navigated the pressures of war, blockade, and neutrality.

Chapter VI — The Middle East and North Africa

The sudden dismissal by the King of the Nahas Pasha Wafd Ministry and the Conference of Arab States at Alexandria in September were the two outstanding events in Egypt. Palestine and Transjordan experienced continued tension. Syria and the Lebanon moved towards independence. The Alexandria Protocol laid the foundation for the Arab League. Iraq and Iran faced strategic pressures.

Chapter VII — The Far East

Foreign and especially American observers in China grew highly critical of the Chiang Kai-shek regime and the Kuomintang, citing inefficiency and corruption. The Japanese offensive in China continued with the capture of Changsha, Hengyang, and advancing on Kweilin. The Allied counter-offensive in the Pacific gathered pace. Japan’s position deteriorated under mounting naval and air losses.

Chapter VIII — America

The United States’ course in 1944 reproduced the rhythm of the total Allied war effort. The first half was a period of preparation and tactical manoeuvre in the Presidential contest. The Republicans nominated Mr. Dewey; the Democrats renominated President Roosevelt for a fourth term. The Bretton Woods Conference established the International Monetary Fund and the World Bank. The Dumbarton Oaks Conference drafted proposals for the United Nations organisation. Argentina, Bolivia, Brazil, Chile, and other Latin American states dealt with pressures of hemispheric solidarity and alignment with the United Nations.
